Ask Bob

A dollar received today is worth more than a dollar received a year from now — not because of inflation, but because today's dollar can be put to work immediately. This principle, the time value of money, is fundamental to every serious capital budgeting decision.

These questions show how time value of money tools apply to the decisions businesses face most often: whether to invest, whether to buy or lease, what discount rate to use, when to replace aging equipment, and how to stress-test your assumptions before committing capital.

The Time Value of Money

1 What is a discount rate, and how do I decide which rate to use? ▼

The discount rate is the rate used to convert future cash flows into their equivalent value today. It reflects the return you would expect from investing your money elsewhere, as well as the risk associated with the investment.

For small and medium-sized businesses, the discount rate might be based on:

  • The cost of borrowed money.
  • The expected return from alternative investments.
  • The level of risk associated with the proposed investment.
2 What is Net Present Value (NPV) and how can it help me decide whether to purchase the asset? ▼

Net Present Value (NPV) is a method of evaluating an investment by comparing the present value of its expected future cash inflows with its initial cost.

Net Present Value
NPV = PV of cash inflows − Initial investment

Generally:

  • Positive NPV: The investment is financially worthwhile.
  • Negative NPV: The investment is unlikely to return the required return.
  • Zero NPV: The investment is expected to earn exactly the required rate of return.
3 What is the appropriate discount rate when evaluating a fixed asset investment? ▼

The discount rate is the most important assumption in any time-value-of-money analysis. Set it too low and you will accept investments that destroy value; set it too high and you will reject investments that create value.

For most small and mid-sized businesses, the discount rate should reflect the opportunity cost of capital and the risk associated with the investment. The key factors to consider:

  • Cost of borrowing — what interest rate would the business pay to finance this investment?
  • Cost of equity — what return could the owners reasonably expect from alternative uses of their money?
  • Business risk — how certain are the expected future cash flows? Higher uncertainty warrants a higher rate.
  • Opportunity cost — what other investment opportunities are available, and what do they return?
  • Inflation — are projected cash flows in nominal or real (inflation-adjusted) dollars?

Because the discount rate has a significant effect on the result, it is essential to test your analysis across a range of rates. The table below shows how NPV changes for a $100,000 investment generating $30,000 annually for five years:

4 How does the Internal Rate of Return (IRR) differ from the NPV, and do I need both? ▼

The IRR tells you the asset's annual percentage return, while NPV tells you the exact dollar value it adds to your business.

IRR is helpful for comparing the asset against your financing costs or against the return on alternative investments (e.g., "This asset yields 14%, while our financing costs are 12%").

Use both together, but if they ever conflict when comparing two different assets, trust the one with the higher NPV.

5 Are there any limitations I should consider when using the time value of money to evaluate my investment? ▼

Yes. Although TVM is a useful decision-making tool, it relies on estimates and assumptions about the future.

For example:

  • Future cash inflows and operating costs may be difficult to predict accurately.
  • The discount rate selected can significantly affect the NPV calculation.
  • Unexpected repairs, maintenance costs or changes in technology could affect the asset's profitability.
  • Inflation and changes in market conditions may influence future cash flows.

Therefore, you should use realistic estimates, consider different possible outcomes and evaluate your assumptions.
